This is a tricky area. The very concept is a rejection that we are incredibly complex beings, and our perception that we are imperfect — along with the inevitable actions that prove our point — is potentially the source of many of our challenges rather than a recognition of our challenges.

From a common sense perspective, self-improvement is likely an excuse as much as an effort to do things differently. Rather than just changing, it affords us, most often, an external control that will result in a change. Pattabhi Jois' exhortation was 'Practice, all is coming', which I take to mean don't improve, practice. In this case practice means daily dedication to a sweaty, consuming practice that changes how you do almost everything, and gradually makes you a stronger, healthier person.

This potentially means you have improved, but probably just means you removed some of the illusions that had previously clouded your vision. And, at times, if you commit, it may also look like you have wrecked certain aspects of your life — which most likely seems to be the very opposite of self-improvement.

The real downside is that we potentially think we are somehow getting better, which inevitably pulls us into the comparative game. Compared to what? It necessarily has no end. We can never be done, more is required, and the impacts are inevitable and will also likely be perceived as needing to be improved.

The inevitable impacts are:

1. The same critical judgment will be directed to others, including our children.
2. We become convinced of and consumed by our flaws.
3. We lose the ability to be happy.
4. We find in proportional measure methods by which to distract ourselves — often food, drugs, entertainment.
5. Over time and through persistent effort we become blind to our compensating strategies, and very likely perceive ourselves as being inconsistent with what is important, which may lead us to illness.
