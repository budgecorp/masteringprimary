---
title: 'The Difference Between Perceiving and Observing'
description: 'Perception feels like a gift. It is more often a shortcut that lets us skip the work of thinking clearly.'
pubDate: 2026-05-03
draft: true
---

I get caught up in perceiving, which seems like an interesting and powerful tool, allowing me to understand the forces that happen around me. It allows me to think I am intuitive, that I can make 'gut calls' based on the undercurrent, the unspoken. The concept is attractive and it is an easy habit to fall into because it makes me feel special and in some way gifted. I observe the perceptive reactions of the smart and funny people I have known and I'd like to be more like them.

My experience however is that it makes me weak, and that I may be misunderstanding how those smart and funny people became so perceptive. Being perceptive also seems to absolve me of the need to think clearly, to expend effort in logic, to be patient enough to observe, and then to take action based on an intentional path rather than the easier reactive path. I tend to think my reactions are honest and authentic, however they are more likely to be lazy — an easier alternative to a thoughtful response.

In practice my combative reaction to perceiving someone's aggression or anger may be entirely misplaced since the aggression and anger may have very little to do with me. The current situation may instead be the result of a hangover, a personal issue, or a sore back. My perception is correct; reacting based on that perception would be incorrect.

Observing the situation requires more effort and control and implies the application of intelligence rather than instinct. With practice my hope is the habit of observing and responding becomes instinctive, replacing the perception-based reactions.

We live in an age where the world expects more of us. Even our warriors — see Stanley McChrystal's book — those who I'd think are best served by perceiving and reacting, are expected to respond based on what they observe to be best in any given situation. The best of us have developed the habit of being clear on our intended or preferred outcome, understanding the current situation including making allowances for the million things we don't know about it, and thoughtfully responding in a manner that is most likely to result in a positive outcome.
