## Students

Stand with the 80 people lined up for the Friday 6am led class and you will hear muted chatter in Chinese (which could be Mandarin, Cantonese, or any of a number of dialects that I've been told is being spoken), in Japanese, Spanish, English, Portuguese, French, Norwegian, Swedish, Russian, or a smattering of others. To make it a little more interesting it may also be English spoken with a wide variety of accents.

Pinning down where someone is from is tricky, because the woman that speaks English like the Queen of England can't read the word 'metatarsal' because she is actually Italian and has never seen the word. The guy I've been speaking English with for 2 days is actually a Spanish-speaking Peruvian who ends up speaking French with my son because he has been living in Paris for the last 9 years and speaks perfect Parisian French.

Making it more interesting, new people come both in batches at the beginning of the week, and in a trickle. Given the numbers (must be more than 200), trying to remember names is interesting and I've opted for 'good morning' or 'good afternoon' with a big smile, and hope that the others remember people's names if I need them. So far not a bad strategy.

People who have been around a while generally have dirtier clothes. This is an imperfect indicator since if someone has come regularly they appear to have their 'India' clothes, which probably look well lived in. The true rookies, like us, wear shiny travel clothes, fresh from MEC or the Italian equivalent. The rookies often have a little extra skin on them, but I'll get to that later.

All in all the student body is a great example of an affluent-world global congregation, and it shows that a world-class institution can now be international, unlike the short years ago when they were all American and European.

## Mysorians

Since it isn't possible to define a group of people I'll try some examples to illustrate the people here.

Our kids are playing soccer almost every day at 5:30 until it is too dark to see the ball. They play with a group of older children who are, for the most part, much better at the sport. The field they play in is red dust and rocks with bigger rocks as goal posts. Some kids play with a variety of footwear (flip-flops and some soccer shoes that look like they were made in the 70s) but many of them play in bare feet. Yes, landing on a rock with toughened feet soles still really hurts.

Their soccer ball is on its last legs and recently lost one of its patches but they prefer it over the new ball we bring to the field every day. Cricket is amazingly popular in Mysore (and presumably in most of India) and is played by kids and adults in every available field in Mysore at all times of the day, including in the middle of a soccer game. Picture a dusty rocky field the size of a small soccer field that is used all at the same time by a guy teaching his wife to drive (she tries, imperfectly, to drive around the kids), two overlapping cricket games, and 20 sub-15-year-old kids playing soccer. Somehow all of this happens without arguments (the cricket players have verbal battles but it appears to be part of the game), competition over space, or any serious accidents.

The kids get a lot of attention. Part of this is great — it allows for a lot of interactions that wouldn't otherwise happen. Part of it is marketing — we go back to shop owners who are nice to the kids since it makes the experience more enjoyable. Part of it is worrisome — a lot of men ask my 5-year-old daughter if they can take her back to their village (22,000 girls have gone missing in Karnataka, the province Mysore is in, in the last 10 years) and touch her much more than I'm comfortable with.
