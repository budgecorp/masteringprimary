My practice should provide a baseline level of wellness that makes it unnecessary for the physical obstructions — my tired liver, my sore hips, my poor posture that results in restricted breathing which increases a sense of overwhelm — to drive my behaviour. What it will also do, in my experience, once that initial layer is resolved, is highlight the infinite layers that reside beneath.

So, in practice, the practice should reduce the creation of new karma which will impede my progress, shorten my life, obstruct my path to performing the purpose that God intended.

How effectively I regulate my emotions is, in part, determined by alcohol, food, lack of sleep, addictive behaviours, and the consumption of media. The application of learned skills is critical and, if I had alcohol last night or especially the last few nights, I will be less diligent in how mindfully or skillfully I practice those skills.

So, it is necessary to restrict the addition of new poisons, create new practices to remove old poisons, and develop new skills.

When annoyed, frustrated, angry, sad — it is worthwhile to ask before reacting: could this emotion exist or be exacerbated as a result of me having consumed (eaten, drunk, watched) poisons or engaged in detrimental behaviours (lack of sleep, improper practice)?

Let's say it's Sunday after two nights of poison and detrimental behaviours, and you receive a demanding email from someone who you perceive as both arrogant and ineffective in areas where you are more effective, and your initial inclination is to punch back.

## The steps of practice

1. **Start where you are at.** This usually means feel the feeling. It is unlikely that you will respond in a manner you perceive as sufficiently sophisticated without experiencing your actual reaction. Fundamental to any yoga practice is to respect where you are at — the body, and many relationships, will quickly let us know where we are practicing self-deception. Iyengar explains well how the mind will constantly try to skip steps, trying to protect itself through the appearance rather than substance of progress.

2. **Engage in the experience well.** This involves two steps: (1) figuring out the best way to engage to achieve the desired outcome, and (2) deciding how we want the person(s) we are interacting with to feel at the end of the interaction. Your practice will teach you through pain and challenge where needed. In yoga this shows up as injury, or in life as challenges that can be linked back to pushing on the mat.

3. **One thing.** Recognizing our opportunities for effective engagement with life are infinite, we are advised to figure out the one thing that will best suit the moment, and do this over and over until it is automatic and layered on top of the thousands of other things that have been previously layered. On the mat, at a macro level, this is the breath, drishti (eye focus), bandhas (energy locks), the uplift of our arches, the down pressure of the rest of our feet, the engagement of our quads, extension through the crown of our head, creation of 'space' between our vertebrae — the list is endless, each added through methodical application of attention over a long period. The practice is to add these with the meta-skills of care, warmth, love, and intention informed by the tens of thousands of practitioners who have followed this path previously.

## Back to the colleague and the awkward email

- Goal is to accomplish a business task, so all action should flow from here. On the mat we will often find ourselves in combat with our ego, our teacher, the practice, the person on the next mat, something that happened during the day. Clearly not the intent of our practice — and we need to shift, usually through surrendering to our bodies and the practice, letting these rather than our ego direct our effort.
- Our combat — that inclination to punch back in this case — often provides guidance for something else we need to resolve. These usually come in three flavours: (1) poisons or ineffective practice reduction, (2) managing the conditions of our experience (temperature, time of day, mat we are using), all of which seem meaningful but are mostly resistance, (3) conditions currently outside of our control (what is in our control expands over time as we become more skillful).
- The difference between the mat and this situation seems significant since the situation is occurring in relationship to others, however this is a bit misleading as our most complex relationship is with ourselves, so learning how to engage effectively with others is often the same as learning how to do this with ourselves.
- In engaging with ourselves on the mat, aggression, anger, fear-driven actions inevitably lead to injury, and it is safe to assume that engaging with this challenge from this emotion will also result in injury. It is worth noting that injury to others creates karma, and inevitably injury to ourselves.
- So, recognizing the goal is to resolve the intended business outcome, that skillful engagement does not create debris, and that this is an opportunity to practice how I engage with myself as well as others — a kind-hearted approach within boundaries that accept where I am at is the right approach.

This will look like:

- Using this as an opportunity to complete the next steps in the task.
- Increase clarity to reduce the likelihood of later similar ineffective engagement.
- Figure out how to give my colleague what they want, which I expect is more authority.
