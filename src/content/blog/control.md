We try to control everything.

From a physical perspective we try to control how we look, smell, how our skin feels. From a habit perspective we try to control how we work, eat, sleep. From a personal level we try to control how we react, treat others, think. From an interpersonal level we try to control how others perceive us, like us, respond to us. And lastly we try to control how we feel, how long we live, and the intangible but most intrinsic sense of our lives.

What if our assumption that we can control any of these things is an illusion?

It often feels like it is. We realize we have toilet paper on our shoe, that the clothes we are wearing make us look sloppy and frail, our spouse tells us that we often have bad breath, we have eczema that we've never figured out how to banish. Our work load is dependent on our current projects; our food often based on our circumstances; and when things are busy at work, the kids are sick, we have been ignoring our spouse and need to spend more time together — our sleep is compromised. People around us are stressed because their kids are sick or their spouses don't like them, they happen to be racist but we didn't know, they have arthritis which leaves them sore, scared and angry. We are getting sick from the lead in our water, we get hit by a car, a loved one has a heart attack no one saw coming, or we just happen to be born in a ghetto full of violence and poverty, or a suburb full of television and shopping.

We can respond with an attempt at more control — there are a lot of self-help books and videos to guide us. We can numb ourselves and drink, drug, eat, or fill our time with other external stimulus. Or we can get someone else to convince us that we are worthy: our kids, our spouse, our co-workers.
