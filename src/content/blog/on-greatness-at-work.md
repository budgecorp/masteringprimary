The people I know who are great at work seem keen to be doing the work, and I assume it's because there is significant value in it for them. Most people are not great at work. Some people who have success at work are not great at work, and success may be temporary.

The people who are great work hard, are willing to dig deep, will do this consistently, are goal-oriented, and are willing to forego small satisfactions to achieve those goals — even when it hurts a little.

People who are great are consistently those who acknowledge what's on their plate, get things done, and communicate clearly around their work.

I've been trying for years not to bring my whole self to work, because that self is angry, disappointed, frustrated, interested in being right, and deeply invested in satisfying my current irritation.

I crave time alone, want to rest or expend effort, and dream of long journeys of self-exploration. I'd like to think I want these so I can experience god.

Tomorrow I will run, write for 10 minutes, do my MW, go to work with a decent breakfast, work hard on one thing till done, be kind to others, leave work between 5 and 6, eat well, row at night, get to bed early, feel love, ask and follow RSF throughout the day.

Deciding the one thing you are going to get done today, and then doing it, and then recognizing that you did it. I think that might be one of the two key drivers of happiness — when the second driver is a positive vision. If it is on repeat. So I'm going to put it on repeat.

The positive vision is that I learn to accomplish an outcome that I decide to accomplish. Ideally it'd be tied to a broader compelling goal but, I think, it might not matter. On Sunday it might just be trim the hedge — which is tied to a broader goal, but likely not compelling.

---

How do you work with someone on something where they haven't done a good job and may not recognize they didn't do a good job?

The initial step is to decide on the persona you are inhabiting and the way you expect that persona to behave. I'd like to say 'be kind' is the necessary metaskill but in some cases being kind might require that you be a little harsh. Saying this, it might just be that I'm not sophisticated enough to provide direct feedback while also being kind.

I learned that expressing my emotions when communicating what I want or need was important. I have not enjoyed people who communicate disappointment in a dispassionate way, perceiving it as arrogance, a lack of respect, or at least a sense of better-than.

Today I provided someone with feedback on some very poorly done work. I started with what I thought was positive, and then dug into why the current material that he'd produced didn't meet my needs. He chose to explain why it was done the way it was, and he had a difficult time acknowledging that there is no valid excuse for poorly done work. I didn't get angry or particularly frustrated but he would have recognized that I wasn't happy from the tone of my voice. If I did this in a sophisticated way, would my voice have indicated how I was feeling about the work and his response? If I over-managed my displeasure when I expressed myself, would he have sufficiently absorbed the message and recognized the need to improve?
