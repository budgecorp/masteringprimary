There have been a few times where I have wanted something and then made a conscious decision to achieve it. These efforts tended to be only vaguely conscious, didn't involve much directed effort, are exceptions in a long list of things I committed to achieving, and may not be consistent with what I actually wanted but were rather something I could live with rather than truly desired.

As an example, my parents wanted me to get into any university that I wanted. I didn't qualify this, but it essentially meant any Canadian university that I wanted because, to be honest, I really wanted to go to Harvard or West Point. It also wasn't an aspiration to have a great university experience or to get into a course that I was really motivated around — it was the minimum of getting into a university. The result was I got into the university I wanted, arguably the most selective one in Canada, but I didn't even try to pick a program of interest, and it felt that I achieved the minimum required by my intention.

My job is a similar outcome. I picked a job that would afford me some career pride but I really just picked a job I could live with — not one that excited or interested me, but one that was the most interesting of the limited options that I saw in front of me.

Where I've arrived with this approach is the place that many people arrive at: I am mildly depressed, I rarely experience excitement or moments of exhilaration, and when I do they are largely manufactured (parachuted out of a plane) or they are the fodder available to someone at my stage in life (a great moment with my wife). These are moments that are expected and to some extent inevitable, if you haven't blown up (potentially in search of something more) your life by middle age. They are also moments on a well-worn path that will be reinforced by another 30 years of good TV, slightly memorable career moments, great food, a series of increasingly challenging health issues — that will leave us increasingly hollowed out and unable or unwilling to fashion the luscious moments of happiness and self-worth that are only possible through a profound connection to the pith of life. Which, I think, is only possible through a continuous commitment to an overarching philosophy that considers our bodies, our minds, our work lives, and the elements in between.

I've spent 30 years recognizing possibility but not being able to bring myself to really bring to life that possibility. Like many people I did the minimum required to not want to slit my wrist, and in doing so I got some things right but not enough to be happy or satisfied. It feels like quiet desperation. It also feels like it will fall apart, and that the breakdown, when it comes, will be deserved and a natural consequence of not doing what I need to do.

I've been practicing yoga since 1993, spent almost a year at an ashram in 1995, went to India to practice Ashtanga yoga with Pattabhi Jois (the originator of Ashtanga yoga) in '96, have been practicing on and off for almost 20 years, and I still haven't mastered the primary series (Ashtanga has 4–6 series, depending on who you ask). Sad as it is, this means that after 20 years of practicing Ashtanga, at a couple of points on a daily basis, I still can't sit in lotus, and I can't complete what in the Ashtanga world is considered the beginner postures.

In 2013, in part to do something about this, I took 6 months off work and went back to India and to Bali and practiced yoga every day. I lost 40 pounds of fat, felt a lot better, but I was still a long way from mastering the primary series. I thought this effort would be the start of a new path and I would continue to chip away at my 20-year goal of mastering primary, but instead I worked long days, ate poorly, and ended up putting on 30 pounds, getting very stiff, and a year and a half later feel as bad as before we left on our family adventure.

Part of what I've realized over the last many months is that mastering primary isn't just about yoga. I have a strong impression that my mastery of many parts of my life is languishing in a similar fashion. I'll use typing as an example, because I spend lots of time typing every day for my work. I can probably type 40 words per minute but I type with a lot of errors. As a knowledge worker I suggest that typing at 50 words per minute is a minimum requirement, and I'm not there. As a second example, I have very little mastery over the breadth of my work. Reading Getting Things Done was a revelation to me but I read it 5 years ago and I still have 300 emails in my inbox, I'm not very clear on the work I need to do next week, never mind 3 weeks out, and I don't have all of my work defined in a single location to allow for easy completion of work during the day. I often end the day feeling like I missed out on completing what I should have completed to move my goals forward.

Paraphrasing BKS Iyengar (one of the grandfathers of modern yoga): "If there is something impeding your spiritual life or practice, then it is a disease and at some point it will manifest itself as illness." If you take this to all aspects of your life, as yoga philosophy tends to allow you to do, then anything that impedes the majestic vision of your life and how you operate within it is a disease and if you don't address it, you will at some point pay a price for it. You don't need to try too hard to get this one — it has a distinct lack of the kung fu mystery flavour. If you sit on your ass watching TV all day you will get fat, uninspired, your work will suffer, and when you need to go through some genuinely uncomfortable moments or periods in your life you will inevitably not have the requisite fortitude to make it through with grace.

Yoga offers a complex and, as I understand it, complete philosophy by which to develop a life of purpose with grace and joy. I intend to find out if this is true, and I'm writing this blog to record my progress as I attempt to follow my imperfect understanding of the yogic philosophy to master what I see as the primary building blocks of my life — with the Ashtanga primary series being my primary learning vehicle and measure of progress.

I'm sure there will be many missteps. Since 6 months of uninterrupted focus did not get me close to mastering the Ashtanga primary series, I am sure this will be a challenging endeavour that clearly requires more than I currently have to offer.

Should be interesting.
